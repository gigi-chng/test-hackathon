# Airtable / Bending Spoons — Narrative Brief

**Purpose:** context and structure for a Slow piece on the Airtable acquisition.
**Status:** draft for review. Every quote and link below is sourced from the
content library; nothing is paraphrased from memory.

---

## 1. What happened

Airtable sold to Bending Spoons. The company peaked around an **$11B**
valuation; the sale is roughly a **10x markdown from that peak**.

The structure has two halves. The legacy Airtable business goes to Bending
Spoons, an operator that buys mature internet assets and runs them for cash.
Founder Howie Liu moves on to a new AI company, with existing investors taking
a slice of it.

Slow is a seed investor. **We put in $3M at a $90M post in 2012** — further
along and a bigger check than we normally do — and **sold portions across the
later rounds.** We made money on it.

---

## 2. Why it matters

Three reasons, in ascending order of interest to a reader:

1. **It's the largest, cleanest example yet of the SaaS repricing** moving from
   public comps into private outcomes. Public software multiples compressed
   first. This is the private mark catching up.
2. **The structure is going to become common.** Sell the legacy business to an
   operator, keep the team for the AI company, give investors exposure to the
   new entity. Expect to see this repeatedly over the next 18 months.
3. **It exposes the gap between marks and money.** An $11B mark and a ~10x
   markdown are the same asset. Which number you experienced depends entirely
   on entry price and whether you ever converted a mark into cash.

Point 3 is the piece. Points 1 and 2 are setup.

---

## 3. Slow's position — disclose in the first two sentences

We are Airtable investors. Saying so up front is the difference between a
credible argument and a discovered conflict. The specifics are favorable:

- In at **$90M post**, not at $11B
- **Sold across later rounds** — converted marks to cash on the way up
- Sam, separately, is a **Bending Spoons shareholder** who bought at IPO

That last one is worth surfacing in the same breath. Said plainly it reads as
"we understood both ends of this trade." Discovered later it reads as talking
your book twice.

## 5. The through-line

> Yoni explained why the asset was getting worse. Will explained why the mark
> wouldn't reflect it. Airtable is where both land at once — and we'd been
> hitting bids since 2012.

Three beats:

1. **The category compressed** (Yoni, Feb–Mar) — software margins are
   permanently dented, and pure application software is the most exposed
2. **The mark didn't move** (Will, Jan–Aug) — because nobody in the chain is
   paid to move it. Marks generate fees and flatter NAV
3. **So you take the bid** (Sam, Airtable) — in at $90M, sold across rounds,
   paid long before the peak mattered

Land on the general lesson: **entry price and realized liquidity determine
outcomes. The peak mark is a number, not a result.**

## 6. Guardrails

**Don't claim we called this deal.** Yoni wrote that the *category* was
compressing. Will wrote about *industry incentives*. Neither wrote "sell
Airtable." The defensible claim is "we had a framework and we acted on it."
The receipts are strong enough that stretching them only creates risk.

**Don't dunk on Airtable or Howie Liu.** He's a founder we backed. The argument
is about LP incentives and mark discipline, not about whether Airtable was a
good company. It was.

**Decide how pointed to be about co-investors.** Will's *"marks generate fees
and make NAV look good"* is a direct shot at how most of the industry operates.
It is the line that gives the piece teeth and the line most likely to irritate
a future syndicate partner. That's a judgment call, not a copy edit.

**Numbers to use:** $3M at $90M post, 2012, ~$11B peak, roughly 10x markdown.
**Number to avoid:** the exact sale price — it was never pinned down on the pod
and reporting has varied.
